SOC 2 Evidence Automation: Quarterly Access Review as a Continuous Control

A company runs its quarterly access review the way most companies still do: an analyst pulls two exports, reconciles them by hand, and hopes nothing slipped through in the three months between reviews. This design document walks through what happens when that control gets rebuilt as a continuous, automated check under SOC 2 CC6, and where the automation has to stop and hand the decision back to a person.

The company below is built for this walkthrough. The SOC 2 Trust Services Criteria referenced are real and current (AICPA, 2017 framework as revised 2022).

Executive Summary

Meridian Data Systems runs its quarterly user access review the way most companies still do: a control owner pulls an export from the identity provider, pulls a separate export from HR, and reconciles the two by hand in a spreadsheet. The review takes two to three days every quarter, covers a point-in-time snapshot, and catches a terminated employee's lingering access only if the reconciliation happens to surface it before the auditor asks.

This document designs the same control as a continuous, automated check under SOC 2 CC6, and then does the part that matters more: it draws the line between what the automation can safely do on its own and what still needs a GRC analyst to decide. The automation is the easy half. The judgment is the reason the control holds up under audit.

The Control Today: Manual, Quarterly, Sampled

A typical quarterly access review works like this. The control owner exports active user accounts from the identity provider (Okta, Entra ID, or equivalent), exports the current employee roster from the HRIS, and manually cross-references the two lists. Anyone in the identity provider export who is not an active employee is a finding. Anyone whose role changed but whose access did not is a finding the manual process usually misses, because role-to-access mapping is rarely reconciled at the same time.

The control works, but it is expensive, it runs once a quarter instead of continuously, and it depends on the reviewer catching what the spreadsheet does not flag on its own. An auditor testing this control is testing four snapshots a year, not the state of access in between.

What Automation Actually Replaces

The reconciliation itself is a data problem, and data problems are exactly what automation is good at. An automated version of this control would query the identity provider and the HRIS on a schedule (daily, not quarterly), cross-reference them programmatically, and produce a timestamped, machine-readable evidence artifact on every run instead of once a quarter by hand.

rule: terminated_user_active_access
  source_a: hris.active_employees
  source_b: idp.active_accounts
  logic: |
    for each account in source_b:
      if account.user_id not in source_a.active_employee_ids:
        flag(account, reason="active IdP account, no matching active employee")
      elif account.role_last_synced < account.hris_role_updated:
        flag(account, reason="role changed in HRIS, access not yet reconciled")
  evidence_output: timestamped_csv(flagged_accounts, run_id, pass_fail_per_account)
  on_fail: route_to(control_owner, sla_hours=48)

Run daily instead of quarterly, this closes the gap between a termination and an access review by up to ninety days, and it produces four times a year's worth of point-in-time evidence as a running log instead of a single spreadsheet an auditor has to trust was done correctly.

Where the Automation Stops and the Analyst Starts

None of the design above answers the questions that actually make this control defensible. Those questions are not technical, and a script cannot resolve them.

What the automation absorbs

  • Pulling and reconciling identity and HR data on a schedule
  • Flagging accounts that fail a defined rule
  • Producing a timestamped, consistent evidence artifact every run
  • Routing a flagged account to an owner within an SLA
  • Running continuously instead of once a quarter

What still needs the analyst

  • Deciding whether a flagged account is a real finding or a legitimate exception (a shared service account, a contractor with a documented extension, a break-glass grant)
  • Setting the rule's risk tolerance: too strict and the control drowns the owner in false positives until they stop reading the queue; too loose and it misses what it exists to catch
  • Writing the control narrative the auditor actually tests: not "the script runs," but why the script's logic satisfies the control's intent
  • Owning the exception population: every account the rule cannot classify becomes a judgment call, and the population of unclassifiable accounts is usually where the real risk sits
  • Deciding what happens when the automation itself fails silently, since a control that only checks access and never checks whether it is still running is a new, undocumented risk

Residual Risk

Automating the reconciliation does not remove the risk of a bad rule. A rule tuned to reduce noise can just as easily suppress a real finding, and a rule this central to the control needs its own periodic review, ideally by someone other than the person who wrote it. The automation also introduces a new dependency: if the identity provider or HRIS integration breaks, the control goes silent rather than late, which is worse, since a late manual review is visible and a silently broken automated one is not. A design without a monitor on the monitor is not finished.

Recommendation

Build the automated reconciliation as designed above, with a companion check that alerts if the automation itself has not produced output in the expected window. Keep a human owner accountable for every exception the rule surfaces and for a quarterly review of the rule logic itself, not just its output. The control gets faster and more consistent. It does not get less dependent on the person running it, it depends on that person in a different place.

What Actually Changes for the Analyst

The reconciliation used to take an analyst two to three days a quarter. A script now does it every day, without a coffee break, and does it better than the manual version did: it never forgets to check the second export. That part of the job is not shrinking. It is gone.

What is left is the part a script cannot inherit: deciding what the rule should flag, defending why that logic satisfies the control's intent when an auditor asks, and owning the exceptions the rule cannot resolve on its own. An analyst who only knew how to run the old manual process now has less to do. An analyst who can design the rule, defend it, and take responsibility for its exceptions has a harder job than before, not an easier one. That is the actual case for keeping a person in this control, not a claim that automation cannot be trusted.

Key Takeaways

  • Automating a control removes the analyst's routine reconciliation work. It does not remove the analyst's judgment about what a flagged account actually means.
  • An automated control needs a monitor on the monitor: a check that alerts when the automation itself goes silent, not just when it finds a bad account.
  • The narrative an auditor tests is why a rule's logic satisfies the control's intent, not confirmation that a script ran on schedule.
  • Every automated rule needs a named owner accountable for its exceptions and for reviewing the rule itself, not only its output.
