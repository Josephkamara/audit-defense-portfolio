ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Statement of Applicability: Justifying Control Exclusions for a Remote-First SaaS Company

A workflow automation company is six months into its first ISO 27001 certification push, driven by three enterprise deals that will not close without it. The draft Statement of Applicability an outside consultant delivered marks all 93 Annex A controls "Applicable." That looks thorough. It is actually the first thing a certification auditor will flag, because a company with no data center, no outsourced developers, and a single leased office does not have 93 controls that apply the same way a bank does. This document rebuilds the SoA from the actual infrastructure up: which controls apply in full, which apply with the scope narrowed to what the company actually operates, and which do not apply at all, with the reasoning an auditor can test.

The company below is built for this walkthrough. The Annex A control numbers, titles, and Clause 6.1.3 requirements cited are real and current (ISO/IEC 27001:2022).

Statement of Applicability Information

SoA ID: ISO-SOA-2026-01

Prepared By: Joseph Kamara, CPA, CISSP, CISA
ISMS Owner: Director of Information Security
Framework: ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Clause 6.1.3 (Information Security Risk Treatment); Annex A, 93 controls across four themes (Organizational, People, Physical, Technological)

Executive Summary

Meridian Workflow Systems builds operations automation software for mid-market finance and healthcare teams, roughly 140 employees, remote-first, one leased office suite for local staff, no owned servers or data centers. All production infrastructure runs on AWS. All product development is done in-house. Three enterprise prospects have told Meridian's sales team that ISO 27001 certification is a contract requirement, not a preference.

The SoA a consulting firm delivered as a starting point marks all 93 Annex A controls "Applicable," with a one-line implementation note for each. It was built from a template, not from Meridian's actual environment. This document replaces it with an SoA built the way Clause 6.1.3(d) requires: the controls that are necessary, a justification for each inclusion and exclusion, and an honest account of what changes if Meridian's infrastructure changes.

The 93/93 Problem

Marking every Annex A control "Applicable" feels safer than excluding anything. It is not. An SoA that shows 93 out of 93 applicable to a 140-person SaaS company with no physical data center tells a certification auditor one of two things: either the company operates infrastructure the auditor has not been told about, or nobody actually reviewed the list against the business before signing it. Both conclusions extend the audit and invite closer scrutiny of everything else in the ISMS.

The opposite failure is just as common and just as fatal to an audit. A remote-first company excludes the entire Physical theme with a single justification: "not applicable, we operate remotely." That statement is false on its face. Employees still use laptops, in homes, at the leased office, in coffee shops. Physical risk does not disappear because there is no server room. It moves to wherever the endpoint is. Annex A already accounts for this: A.7.9 (Security of assets off-premises) and A.6.7 (Remote working) exist specifically to cover it. A blanket physical exclusion does not read as judgment. It reads as a company that has not thought through where its risk actually lives.

What Meridian's ISMS Actually Has to Cover

Three facts about Meridian's operating model drive almost every exclusion and scope modification in this SoA: production infrastructure is 100% AWS IaaS and PaaS under an enterprise agreement, no company-owned server or storage hardware exists anywhere, and all product development is performed by employees, with no outsourced development firm holding source code access as of this SoA's effective date. Where a control exists to manage a risk Meridian has moved to AWS under the shared responsibility model, or a risk that does not exist because the underlying activity does not happen at Meridian, that reasoning appears in the tables below with the specific control it replaces or the specific fact that makes it inapplicable.

One Technological control is applicable without modification but worth naming directly: A.8.33 (Test Information). Meridian's test and staging environments use synthetic data only. Production customer data is never copied into a non-production environment. That is not an exclusion. It is a control decision that makes several other risks smaller, and it is the kind of detail an SoA line item does not show unless someone writes it down.

What an Auditor Will Push Back On

A checked box and a paragraph of justification are not the same evidence, and a certification auditor is trained to tell the difference.

What the checkbox shows

  • "Applicable" or "Not Applicable" next to a control number
  • A one-line reference to a policy document that mentions the control
  • A control mapped to a framework name in a spreadsheet
  • A completion percentage on a compliance dashboard

What the justification text has to prove

  • Which specific piece of infrastructure, location, or process the exclusion applies to, not a general description like "cloud" or "remote"
  • What compensating control or supplier attestation covers the risk the excluded control would otherwise have addressed
  • When the justification stops being true, and who is responsible for catching that before the next scheduled review
  • Enough specificity that an auditor can verify the claim against evidence, rather than take the sentence on faith

Residual Risk Statement

This SoA reflects Meridian's infrastructure and operating model as of September 2026: fully cloud-hosted, one leased office, in-house development only. Two of those facts are structural and unlikely to change on short notice. The third is not. If Meridian contracts an outside development team, for a single product line or to meet a deadline, A.8.30 becomes applicable the day that contract is signed, not at the next annual SoA review. The exclusion holds only because Procurement is required to flag any new vendor granted source code access to the ISMS owner immediately, a control point documented under A.5.19, not because the SoA says so once a year.

Recommendation

Approve this Statement of Applicability as the basis for Stage 1 certification audit readiness. Assign an owner to document the office's specific physical setup, the single network closet, the card-key perimeter, the landlord-operated monitoring, in Meridian's Physical Security Procedure before the Stage 1 audit, so the scope-modified controls above have a supporting document an auditor can read. Confirm AWS's current ISO/IEC 27001 certificate and SOC 2 Type II report are on file and less than twelve months old before citing them as evidence for A.7.11 and A.7.12. Re-run this exclusion analysis at the next material change to infrastructure, vendor relationships, or office footprint, not only at the annual review date.

What Eight Rows of Justification Are Actually For

Ninety-three applicable controls and three not-applicable controls are both, by themselves, just a number. Neither tells an auditor whether the person who wrote the SoA looked at Meridian's actual infrastructure or copied a template. The eight rows in this document that mark a control excluded or scope-narrowed are the only part doing real work, because each one names the specific piece of infrastructure, contract, or process the exclusion depends on.

That specificity is the difference between compliance and a document that describes compliance. A.8.30's exclusion for outsourced development holds only as long as Procurement flags a new vendor with source code access to the ISMS owner the day that contract is signed, and the SoA says so directly instead of hiding behind an annual review date. A control that traces to a named piece of Meridian's actual environment survives an auditor's questions. A control that traces to nothing more than the words "cloud" and "remote" does not.

Key Takeaways

  • Marking every control "Applicable" reads as unreviewed, not thorough. Excluding an entire theme with one blanket line reads as risk nobody traced.
  • Every exclusion or scope narrowing needs to name the specific infrastructure, contract, or process it depends on, and state when it stops being true.
  • Organizational and People controls apply regardless of infrastructure. Physical and Technological controls are where the real judgment calls concentrate.
  • A control that traces to a named piece of the actual environment survives an auditor's questions. One that traces only to words like "cloud" or "remote" does not.
